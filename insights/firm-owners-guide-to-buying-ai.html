Buying AI

The Firm Owner’s Guide to Buying AI

By Nico Castagnola · August 2026 · 6 min read

For ten years, my job has been standing in front of skeptical buyers while they decided whether my company’s software was real. Not whether the demo was impressive. Demos are always impressive; that’s what demos are for. Whether it was real: on their data, in their environment, at their volume, handled by their people.

The mechanism enterprises use to answer that question is the proof of concept. Before a serious buyer commits serious money, they make the vendor prove the product on a slice of the buyer’s actual problem, against criteria written down in advance, on a clock. I’ve run hundreds of these evaluations from the vendor side. I have watched deals die because a product that demoed beautifully couldn’t survive contact with real documents, real edge cases, real Tuesdays.

Here is what strikes me now, watching law and accounting firms get pitched AI: almost nobody makes AI vendors and consultants earn it the way enterprise buyers made us earn it. The typical pitch starts with a vision deck and a retainer, and ends, months later, with something that quietly doesn’t get used. The failure usually isn’t the technology. It’s the buying process.

What the proof discipline actually is

Strip away the enterprise procurement theater and a good evaluation has four parts:

1. Success criteria, in writing, before anything is built. Not “we’ll know it when we see it.” Numbers: an engagement letter takes 45 minutes to produce today; the automation must get a reviewable draft under 5, with zero stale client details surviving from the template. If a seller can’t write the criteria, they don’t understand your process yet. That’s worth learning before money moves.

2. Real work, not demo data. Every product works on the demo dataset. The demo dataset was chosen because the product works on it. The only test that matters runs on your actual intake, your inconsistent client documents, your exceptions.

3. A clock. Open-ended evaluations don’t conclude; they dissolve. Thirty to forty-five days proves almost any well-scoped automation. If someone needs six months to show value, the scope is wrong.

4. A decision rule. Pass means scale. Miss means stop. Agreed in advance, so nobody relitigates it under the influence of sunk costs and enthusiasm.

Why AI sellers avoid this structure

Because it’s bad for them and good for you. A consultant paid monthly to “drive AI transformation” has no incentive to reach a pass/fail moment; the ambiguity is the business model. A structure with written criteria and a scheduled verdict transfers risk from the buyer to the seller. That’s exactly why you should insist on it, and why a seller’s reaction to the request is itself a signal. The ones who flinch were planning to sell you confidence. The ones who say “great, here’s how I’d structure the criteria” have done this before.

Five questions to ask anyone selling your firm AI

You don’t need to be technical to run this gauntlet. Ask these, and listen for numbers in the answers:

“What will we measure, and what’s the baseline today?” If they haven’t asked to watch your current process, they can’t answer honestly.

“What happens if it misses the criteria?” The right answer involves you stopping, keeping the work products, and understanding why. The wrong answer is a pivot to how iteration is part of the journey.

“Will it run on our real client work during the evaluation?” An evaluation on sample data is a demo with a longer invoice.

“Where does our client data go?” You are entitled to a written answer naming what data the system touches, where it is processed, what leaves your systems, and which AI providers see it under what terms. For a firm bound by privilege or professional confidentiality, a hand-wave here ends the conversation.

“Who maintains this in month six?” Automations aren’t paintings; they’re plumbing. Document formats drift, tools change, volume grows. Someone must own that, in writing.

The part nobody tells you: a failed pilot is a win

In enterprise evaluations, a proof of concept that fails fast is a success. It just saved the buyer a seven-figure mistake at a five-figure price. The same math holds at firm scale. A 30-day build that proves your intake process isn’t ready for automation (usually because the upstream information is chaos) is cheap tuition, and it tells you exactly what to fix first. The expensive version of that lesson is the same discovery in month eight of a retainer.

The AI wave is real, and professional services work, which is mostly documents, deadlines, and correspondence, is unusually well suited to it. But real technology deserves a real buying process. Demand proof. The sellers worth hiring will thank you for it.

This is how I structure every engagement

Written success criteria, real client work, a scheduled verdict, and your call at the end. That’s the standard, because I spent a decade on the other side of it.
