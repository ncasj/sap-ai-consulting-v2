Ten Automations a Law or Accounting Firm Can Ship in 30 Days

By Nico Castagnola · August 2026 · 8 min read

When firm owners imagine AI, they imagine the moonshot: a robot lawyer, an AI accountant. What actually pays back, over and over, is far less cinematic. It’s the high-volume glue work that connects your systems and eats your staff’s week twenty minutes at a time, and it happens that professional services work (documents, deadlines, correspondence) is the single best category of business for this technology that I have ever seen.

These are ten automations I consider proven at the 5-to-50-person firm scale. Each is genuinely shippable in about 30 days. For each: what it does, why it pays, and, because honesty is the house rule, where it gets hard.

1. Client intake orchestration

One intake form feeds everything: the matter or engagement gets set up in your systems, folders are created by your conventions, screening or conflict checks are queued, and the kickoff gets scheduled. The half-day of setup per new client becomes minutes, and every client’s first week looks identical. Where it gets hard: agreeing internally on what the standard intake even is. The automation forces that conversation, which is a feature.

2. Engagement letters and proposals

First drafts generated from intake data and your own precedents, with current names, scope, and fees filled correctly. Partners review and sign instead of assembling from an old file and hoping nothing stale survives. Where it gets hard: if your precedents are inconsistent, the automation learns your inconsistency. Curate the source set first.

3. Document collection chasing

Clients get a checklist of what to send, automated reminders until it all arrives, and everything filed to the right place on receipt. Your staff stops playing fetch, and busy season starts with complete files. Where it gets hard: clients send the wrong things in the wrong formats. Budget for a review queue rather than pretending exceptions won’t happen.

4. Document intake and filing

Inbound documents read, classified, named by your conventions, and filed to the right matter, with anything ambiguous queued for a person. The shared-drive archaeology ends. Where it gets hard: scanned and photographed documents. Modern models handle them well, but the exception queue has to be honest about the residue.

5. Time capture and invoice drafting

Work activity (calendar, email, documents touched) turned into draft time entries, and invoices assembled from captured time at firm rates, queued for partner approval. Billing stops depending on Friday memory, and bills go out on time. Where it gets hard: partners must still review; auto-billed hours without human sign-off is how trust dies. Design the approval step well and this is the highest-payback automation on the list for most firms.

6. Collections follow-up

Overdue invoices chased with polite, escalating sequences written in your voice, payments reconciled, and only genuine disputes reaching a partner. Most firms are owed real money simply because chasing it is awkward and forgettable. Where it gets hard: tone. These emails go to your clients, so the sequences must sound like you on your best-mannered day, and a person should approve the first month of sends.

7. Client status reporting

“Where do things stand?” answered before it’s asked: status updates drafted from matter or engagement activity on a cadence you choose, reviewed and sent by your team. The single biggest source of inbound interruptions disappears. Where it gets hard: it’s only as good as what your systems capture, which is why this often follows automation #5 rather than leading.

8. Email triage

The firm’s shared inbox (and, carefully, partner inboxes) classified, routed, and answered in draft, grounded in your firm’s knowledge. Nothing sits unanswered over a weekend, and the ten questions that make up most of the volume get consistent, reviewed answers. Where it gets hard: your written knowledge is thinner than you think. The pilot usually reveals which ten answers cover 60% of volume; write those well and the automation flies.

9. Scheduling and follow-ups

Meeting requests, confirmations, reschedules, and post-meeting follow-up notes handled without inbox ping-pong, plus prep briefs compiled before each client meeting so nobody walks in cold. Where it gets hard: honestly, it doesn’t much. This is often the confidence-building first win while a bigger build runs.

10. Deadline and date tracking

Key dates extracted from documents on arrival (filing deadlines, renewal dates, statute periods, tax calendar items) and tracked automatically with escalating reminders. The firm stops relying on whoever happened to read the document. Where it gets hard: this one carries real consequence if it misses, so it runs as a safety net over your existing docketing practice, never as a replacement for it. Extraction drafts, a person confirms.

How to read this list

Notice the pattern: every automation here has high volume, a clear input and output, and a human review point wherever judgment, client contact, or professional responsibility lives. That’s not a coincidence. It’s the profile of automation that survives month six.

And no firm should build all ten at once. The right move is an audit to find which one pays back fastest for your specific practice, then a single implementation with written success criteria to prove it. One proven automation your staff trusts beats five impressive ones they’ve quietly stopped using.
